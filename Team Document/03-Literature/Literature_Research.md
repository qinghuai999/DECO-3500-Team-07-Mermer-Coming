# Literature Research
## Social Compliance / Social Power in Team Gaming



# 1. Starting Point: Social Power Relations Exist Between Players in Multiplayer Games

## Ömerbaş, A. C. (2023) — *The Power Bases in Multiplayer Video Games*

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875952123000447

**Original Evidence — Abstract**

> “This study focuses on measuring the power of a player over another one.”

> “There can be two types of such relations... inter-power relations... or... intra power relations.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This paper gave us a very important theoretical starting point:

- **player-to-player power relations** exist in multiplayer games;
- power does not only occur between opposing players, but can also occur within cooperative teams;
- influence between players may come from different sources, such as:
  - expertise / skill;
  - reward;
  - social relationship / referent power.

Therefore, our initial research interest should not only be:

> “Why do players play for too long?”

Instead, we can further ask:

> **How are influence, compliance, and decision-making power distributed within multiplayer gaming teams?**

### What This Literature Does Not Tell Us

It does not directly tell us:

- which type of power is most obvious in ordinary gaming groups;
- whether quiet players are actually in a weaker position;
- among leaving, evaluating, inviting, organising, choosing modes, etc., which behaviour is most worth studying;
- what kind of product we should design.

These all need to be confirmed through primary research.

### Interview Directions Generated From This

We can start by asking players:

- Who usually starts the gaming session?
- Who usually decides what to play?
- Who most often says “one more game”?
- Is there someone in the group whose opinion others are more likely to follow?
- Why do people listen to that person?
- Have you ever had a different opinion but still ended up following the group decision?

# 2. Literature Theme A: Leaving an Interaction May Itself Be a Form of Social Negotiation

## Hancock et al. (2009) — *Butler Lies: Awareness, Deception and Design*

https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/1518701.1518782

**Original Evidence — Abstract**

> “We introduce the notion of the ‘butler lie’ to describe lies that allow for polite initiation and termination of conversations.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This study shows that in computer-mediated communication, people sometimes do not start or end an interaction in a very direct way.

They may use an indirect explanation / excuse to:

- manage their own availability;
- politely end a conversation;
- reduce the relational cost of directly refusing or leaving.

This gives us an interesting research direction:

> **In multiplayer gaming, when players want to leave, is there a similar social cost?**

Note that at this stage, we **had not yet thought of an “excuse generator.”**

The literature only made us realise that:

> **disengagement itself may not be a purely individual behaviour, but a social act that needs to be negotiated with the group.**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- When you want to leave before your teammates, what do you usually say?
- Is it easy to directly say “I want to stop”?
- Have you ever made up a reason or excuse?
- When someone says they want to leave, how do the others usually respond?
- Does anyone try to persuade them to keep playing?
- Do people react differently depending on who says they want to leave?

---

## **Yuan, Chien Wen (Tina) & Bi, Nanyi.** **“Too close to lie to you: investigating availability management on multiple communication tools across different social relationships.”**

https://www.emerald.com/lht/article/41/3/877/453019

**Original Evidence — Findings**

> “The practice of availability management and the use of butler lies are negatively moderated by people's conversational grounding and trust.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This paper provides an important limitation to the previous Butler Lies study.

It found that:

> When trust and shared conversational background between people are higher, the use of availability management / butler lies actually decreases.

Simply put:

> **The closer, more trusting, and more familiar people are with each other, the lower the need for traditional “excuses to leave” may be.**

So we cannot directly assume:

> “Players need an excuse when they want to leave.”

Instead, we should further investigate:

> **Does relationship closeness change the way players express that they want to leave a gaming session?**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- Are the people you usually play with real-life friends, online friends, or friends of friends?
- When playing with very close friends, are you more likely to directly say “I’m leaving”?
- When playing with people you are not very close to, do you feel more awkward about directly refusing?
- In what type of relationship are you most likely to feel “everyone else is continuing, so it is hard for me to leave”?

### Tension Formed at the Literature Stage

**Hancock et al.** tells us:

> indirect messages may help people manage disengagement.

But **Yuan & Bi** also tells us:

> in relationships with high trust and familiarity, the need for traditional excuses may decrease.

So what we actually gain at this point is not a design answer, but a research question:

> **How does relationship closeness within a gaming group affect the way players negotiate leaving?**

# 3. Literature Theme B: Social Visibility May Affect Whether People Are Willing to Express Their Real Opinions

## Postmes & Lea (2000) — *Social Processes and Group Decision Making: Anonymity in Group Decision Support Systems*

https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140130050084978

**Original Evidence — Abstract**

> “The only reliable effect of anonymity was to lead to more contributions, especially more critical ones.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This is a meta-analysis.

It does not prove that:

> “Anonymity always makes group decision-making better.”

But it found one relatively consistent result:

> **Anonymity can lead to more contributions, especially more critical contributions.**

This made us start thinking:

> **In gaming groups, are there opinions that players choose not to express because “other people will know it was me who said it”?**

At this stage, we had not yet designed an anonymous player portrait.

We only wanted to understand:

> **Whether identifiability / social visibility affects expression within gaming groups.**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- Would you directly tell a teammate “you played badly this round”?
- Are there things you think but would not say in the group?
- Do you avoid evaluating others because you are afraid of awkwardness or damaging the relationship?
- Are there some people in the group who are more willing to evaluate others?
- If other people did not know who gave the evaluation, would you be more willing to express your opinion?
- Would anonymity make feedback easier, or might it make people harsher?

---

## **Gielen & De Wever (2015) — \*Structuring Peer Assessment: Comparing the Impact of the Degree of Structure on Peer Feedback Content\***

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563215004598

**Original Evidence — Highlights**

> “More structure directs the focus of peer feedback more on particular criteria.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This paper itself comes from educational peer assessment, not gaming.

So it cannot directly prove that:

> “Using a controlled vocabulary in gaming will definitely be effective.”

But it gives us an insight that can be transferred:

> **The structure of a feedback mechanism affects what people actually evaluate.**

More structure can make feedback more focused on specific criteria.

Therefore, before actually designing a controlled vocabulary, we should first confirm with players:

> **When players evaluate each other, are they evaluating gaming behaviour, technical performance, teamwork, or does it easily turn into personal attacks?**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- After a game, do you evaluate each other?
- What do you usually evaluate?
- Skill?
- Teamwork?
- Decision-making?
- Personality?
- Jokes?
- Is there any feedback that feels too personally targeted?
- What kinds of evaluation feel like harmless joking, and what kinds cross the line?

### Tension Formed at the Literature Stage

**Postmes & Lea** makes us focus on:

> **whether visibility / anonymity affects willingness to express opinions.**

**Gielen & De Wever** makes us focus on:

> **whether the structure of expression itself changes the content of feedback.**

So before designing anything, we can form a broader research question:

> **How can players express their real opinions about teammates without making the interaction feel overly personal or allowing it to be dominated by a few stronger members?**

At this point, this is not yet Feature 2.

It is only a social phenomenon worth further investigating through interviews.

# 4. Literature Theme C: Decision-Making Power in Groups May Emerge Naturally or Be Redistributed Through Mechanisms

## **Chen et al. (2023), “Understanding Social Interactions in Location-based Games as Hybrid Spaces: Coordination and Collaboration in Raiding in Pokémon GO”, CHI 2023**

https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3544548.3581544

**Original Evidence — Abstract / Discussion**

> “...spontaneously formed leadership roles...”

The Discussion also mentions:

> “Some high-level and experienced players volunteer to lead the team and organize the Raid Battles.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This gives us a very important piece of gaming-specific evidence:

> Even when there is no formal leader in the system, an organiser / leader may still naturally emerge within a gaming group.

For example:

- experienced players may actively organise activities;
- some people may take responsibility for invitations;
- some people may habitually coordinate the group;
- others may follow their arrangements.

So we began to focus on:

> **Do ordinary multiplayer gaming groups also naturally develop a “default decision-maker”?**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- Who usually organises the group to play?
- Who most often sends invitations?
- Who decides what to play next?
- Why is it usually this person who makes the decision?
- Do other people suggest different options?
- When people disagree, who makes the final decision?

---

## Ertac & Gurdal (2019) — *Preference Communication and Leadership in Group Decision-Making*

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S221480431930117X

**Original Evidence — Abstract**

> “...explores the effects of preference communication and leader selection mechanisms in group decision-making.”

In the experimental mechanism, they also included:

> “A fair procedure (random draw) determines the leader from among willing group members.”

### What the Literature Tells Us

This paper made us realise that:

> **“Who has the right to make a decision on behalf of the group” is itself a variable that can be studied.**

But importantly, we cannot immediately say:

> “Then we should randomly select someone.”

The correct interpretation is:

> **leader-selection mechanisms can affect group interaction, so we should first understand who currently gets the final say in gaming groups.**

### Interview Questions Generated From This

- When the team has different opinions, how do you usually resolve it?
- Is there someone who eventually steps in and says “let’s just do this”?
- Is it usually the same person?
- Why do people accept that person’s decision?
- If someone who is normally not the organiser suddenly made the final decision, would people find it strange?
- What would you think if decision-making opportunities rotated between members?

### Tension Formed at the Literature Stage

**Chen et al.** tells us:

> informal leadership can naturally emerge in gaming contexts.

**Ertac & Gurdal** tells us:

> leader selection mechanisms themselves can be changed and studied.

So the real question we should bring into user research is:

> **How is temporary decision authority usually distributed within a gaming group? How do members feel about “who gets the final say”?**
